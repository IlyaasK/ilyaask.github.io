Part One

Why I am writing this

“The Von Neumann bottleneck stops being a diagram in a textbook and becomes the thing standing between me and my next experiment.” Why I keep coming back to this problem

Hey Truffle Team,

I’m Ilyaas Kapadia, a sophomore in computer science at the University of Cincinnati. I want to intern as a software engineer at Deepshard for Spring 2027. I want to learn, and I want to be useful.

Why Deepshard? Because when I train small RL policies I am often memory bound, and I feel the Von Neumann bottleneck every day — not as a lecture slide, but as the wall between me and the next run. Because I abuse ChatGPT’s Browser Use. Because the agentic future is clear. Because models are getting smaller and more parallel, and the local future is clear. Truffle is the next step forward.

Because my grandparents got to see the transformation from vacuum tubes to the personal computer, and I get to see the same thing happen a second time, and it makes me so excited. I want to contribute to it. To get from time sharing to owning inference, for all. From the Nvidia Jetson on my desk to the Truffle Chip.

While my technical abilities may not be up to scratch yet: what I don’t have in experience, I will make up for in hustle.

What follows is the evidence for that last sentence, then where I would plug in, then the parts of this I am still bad at. If you only read one of them, read the last one.

The shape of the thing I want

A computer in my house that runs an agent all night, on my electricity, with my weights, at a marginal cost near zero — and a chip underneath it that was designed for that job instead of borrowed from a datacenter. That is not a product pitch. It is the reason I read about KV caches and rooflines instead of watching television, and the reason I want to spend a spring semester in Los Angeles instead of applying to ten places with the same cover letter.

Part Two

What I have already done

For an opportunity like this you will have a lot of candidates, and every one of them will tell you they work hard. So here is what I have shipped instead.

$250,000, and the first line of code I ever wrote

My first job ever was as a sales consultant, and I did not know how to sell. I still generated $250,000 for that employer and was the top sales consultant for summer 2024–25. Then I got tired of doing their data analysis by hand, so I wrote code to make it faster. That is where the CS journey started — not with a class, with an irritation.

Kernel: API keys, Terraform, and smaller Chromium images

My first software internship was at Kernel (YC S25), which runs browser infrastructure for agents. My first project there was implementing programmatic API key generation. From there I owned Kernel’s Terraform provider, made their Chromium images smaller and faster to build, and worked on Kernel’s MCP.

I also built a demo where I made an MCP server more efficient — which I could only do by reverse-engineering the API underneath it. That was the most fun I had all summer, and it is why I would love to help Abdullah build DevEx.

Environments: training faster than the game it trains on

After Kernel I wanted to understand pretraining, but RL pulled me in instead. I started with Pufferlib and environment creation, and I fell in love with building environments and simulations — with making them fast enough that you train a policy in seconds instead of days. I built an LLM eval (NES Eval)4. I am learning CUDA to push the training loops further.

Brotato, in twelve hours

Yesterday I joined a hackathon and reverse-engineered Brotato, a game my friends play, so I could build a policy for it. I took a game running on the Godot engine, stripped the engine out, reimplemented the simulation in C, and parallelized the physics and the contact solving in CUDA. Then I wrote a Godot mod that drives the real game headless and records it, so my simulator’s frames can be checked against the game’s frames.

Game training went from about 1,200 steps per second to roughly 1.8 million in my environment. The policy transfers into the simulator; getting it to survive in the real game is the part I have not solved, and frame exactness is still in progress.

Inference, on my own hardware

I read Inference Engineering and helped create recipes for omarchy’s local AI plugin based on my own GPU, because I wanted to know what my card can actually hold and how fast it can actually go.

On Thursday I am building a small robot with an upperclassman friend to validate my RL policy in the real world — partly because the policy in the original project was imitating a scripted bot and I knew pure RL could solve it, and partly because I want MechE and EE skills so I can learn from and help Dylan with firmware and inference, and Hy with embedded systems.

Which is why I would really like to speak to Matt about his work with what seems like post-training small LLMs. I am so curious to see what Truffle’s dreaming is.

Part Four

Where I am weak

None of this is a reason to hire me. It is the list of things I intend to be better at in twelve months, and I would rather get better at them at Deepshard than anywhere else.

CUDA

I have started. Parallel physics in a game simulator is the most complicated thing I have written, and I got there by reading and breaking things rather than by being taught. I cannot yet look at a kernel and tell you why it is slow without a profiler open.

Frame exactness

In progress. My simulator matches the recordings I have for a few thousand ticks; it does not match everything, and until it does I cannot fully trust the training signal.

Sim to real

Not solved. My Brotato policy averages wave 3.08 in my simulator and dies in wave 1 of the real game. The robot project is partly me forcing myself to face that gap with hardware instead of more evaluation runs.

Pretraining, and scale

I have never pretrained a model. I have read a lot about it and I have opinions, which is not the same thing. I have worked on one production codebase, Kernel’s, for a few months. I have never carried a system through years of change.
